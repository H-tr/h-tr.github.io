# Challenges and gaps

The second section says why the question is hard and where the literature is empty.

## Challenges

A challenge is a reason independent of the chosen method.

- Tie each challenge to one part of the [formulation](problem.md#formulation).
- Give one mechanism and one piece of evidence open to a check by the reader. A challenge
  without evidence is an opinion.
- Say what the evidence was measured on. When the evidence comes from another field, state the
  transfer to robots as an argument of the survey, not as a result of the source.
- Give each challenge a short noun phrase as the h3 title.
- Open the paragraph with the claim, then the mechanism, then the evidence.

## Conflicting evidence

- When results disagree on a question of the survey, give the disagreement a subsection.
- Report each side with the same weight, and say what differs between the studies (tasks,
  tools, baselines).
- Do not resolve a disagreement the evidence does not resolve.
- Report evidence against the thesis of the survey.

## Gaps

A gap is a place without published work.

- Run a search for counterexamples before stating a gap. Search for the idea under other
  names. Record the queries and the result in the source index
  ([literature](literature.md#searching)).
- Write a gap as "no work found with the following property", name the nearest work, and say
  what the nearest work lacks. Never write that something "has not been done".
- Narrow a gap when the search finds partial work. Drop a gap when the search finds a full
  counterexample.
- Keep gaps apart from challenges. A challenge can have much published work. A gap can be easy.
- When new work fills a gap, move the work to the solutions and delete the gap.
- State gaps. Do not recommend a project, an experiment or a method to build.
